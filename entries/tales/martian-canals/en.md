## 1. 1877

In 1877 Mars came close to Earth.

At Milan, the Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli observed the planet through a telescope. He drew the light and dark markings and saw thin lines crossing the pale regions, linking the dark patches.

He called them canali. In Italian the word means channels or grooves, not artificial canals.

But in the English press, canali became canals.

That one word changed the story of Mars for the next eighty years.

## 2. Lowell

In 1894 the American businessman Percival Lowell built an observatory at Flagstaff, Arizona.

Lowell had money and an obsession. He believed Mars was inhabited, and that the straight lines were canals dug by Martians. Over more than a decade he drew maps of the planet, adding more and more lines, until he had hundreds of canals.

In his maps the canals ran from the poles across the deserts and met in the dark regions. He argued that Mars was drying out, and that its inhabitants were digging canals to carry water from the poles to farms near the equator.

It was a compelling idea. It turned Mars from a point of light into a world with a history, an engineering project and a crisis.

## 3. Canal Fever

Lowell's books sold well. Newspapers, magazines and novelists began writing about Martians.

In 1898 H. G. Wells published The War of the Worlds, in which Martians invade England in cylindrical spacecraft. In 1912 Edgar Rice Burroughs published A Princess of Mars, turning the planet into a fantasy world of princesses, airships and war.

Canals entered atlases and textbooks. Some astronomers said they could see them too; others said they could not. The argument ran for decades.

## 4. The Critics

Not everyone believed.

The American astronomer Edward Barnard observed Mars through one of the largest telescopes of the day and said he saw not a single canal. The British naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace published a book in 1907, Is Mars Habitable?, rebutting Lowell point by point.

Spectroscopy found no water vapour in the Martian atmosphere. The air was too thin and too cold; even with canals, liquid water could not flow on the surface.

But the critics were not loud enough. The canal story had already left the observatory and entered popular culture.

## 5. The Spacecraft

In July 1965 the American probe Mariner 4 flew past Mars and took twenty-one photographs.

They showed a cratered, Moon-like surface. No canals, no cities, no vegetation.

In 1971 Mariner 9 entered orbit and spent months mapping the whole planet. It found enormous volcanoes, canyons and dry river beds, but not one straight line crossing a continent.

The canals did not exist.

## 6. Why They Saw Them

So why did so many astronomers see canals?

Partly it was the telescopes. Nineteenth-century instruments suffered from chromatic aberration and diffraction, and Mars was a tiny target; turbulence in the air turned details into lines. The eye is good at joining disconnected dots, especially when you already believe the lines are there.

Partly it was translation and transmission. Canali became canals; channels became engineering; natural features became construction.

A mistranslation, an optical illusion and a willing audience were enough to make a whole era see a Mars that was not there.

## 7. Mars

Mars did have water.

Today's probes have found ancient river beds, deltas, lake deposits and water-bearing minerals on the surface. Billions of years ago the planet was warmer and wetter, with rivers and lakes.

But the water flowed naturally. There were no canals, no engineers and no dying civilisation.

The lines Schiaparelli saw were made by the telescopes and minds of his time. He made a mistake, and the mistake produced an entire genre of literature.

Sometimes an error tells a better story than the fact.
